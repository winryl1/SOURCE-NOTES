Logic is one of the [[Philosophy Concept#Branches of Philosophy]] and is the **philosopher's toolbox.** It deals with arguments and uses careful rational thought to arrive at the truth. In logic, our goal is to **use arguments/premises correctly** and avoid [[Fallacies]]. 

## Deductive 
A **deductive argument** is a type of argument that goes from **broad to specific**. It is based on the **assumption** that **all the premises are true** and therefore, the conclusion must also be true. In other words, conclusions in deductive arguments **must be universal** with no room for exception (probabilities: most, or majority). 

In evaluating a deductive argument, there are two concepts needed: 
1. **Validity**: solely depends on the **form** of the argument, not whether the premise are true
2. **Soundness**: An argument can only be "sound" if it is:
	- Valid (in form)
	- All of its **premises** are **actually true** (**fact-based**)
If even one of these concepts are not followed, the argument is **invalid**.
## Inductive
An **inductive argument** is a type of argument that goes from **specific to broad**. Unlike deductive, it based on the **probability** that the conclusion is likely to be true. It **makes a generalization** (or hypothesis, prediction, etc.) based on **specific observations or evidence**. And because of this nature of probability, it is the exact opposite of universality, which means that while the conclusions are likely to be true, they may also be false.

An inductive argument can either be: 
1. **Strong**: when the evidences presented in the **premises are strong** enough to **lead us to believe that the conclusion is likely**
2. **Weak**: when the premises **give little reason to believe the conclusion**, thus failing to make it probable
