
> [!Success] Enzymes Overview
> **Enzymes** are proteins (see [[Proteins & Amino Acids]]) with complex and unique tertiary structures. They are known as **biological catalysts** without being used up in the reaction itself. They speed up the reaction by **lowering** the **activation energy**. 
![[enzyme-activation-energy-comparison.png]]

Enzymes can be grouped into two categories: 
1.  **Intracellular enzymes** - These act within the cells that produce them.
2. **Extracellular enzymes** - These act outside the cells that produce them, and are secreted.

# Models of Action 
> [!important]- Main Point 
Enzymes have unique **tertiary structures** which determine the shape of their active site. Their shape is complementary to the substrate. The substrate binds to the active site where temporary bonds are formed between both R groups. These bonds result in a **lower activation energy** to help break down the substrate into products.
![[enzymes reaction process.jpg]]

## Lock and Key
In this model, the substrate **fits perfectly** into the active site in the same way a key fits into a lock.![[lock-and-key-model-enzyme-action.png]]
## Induced Fit
In this model, the substrate does **not fit perfectly** into the active site. As the substrate enters the enzyme, the active site **changes shape slightly** which puts a strain on the substrate's bonds and lowers the activation energy.
![[induced-fit-model-enzyme-action.png]]

# Factors affecting enzyme action 
1. Temperature
	- All enzymes have an optimum temperature, but these can vary.
	- As temperature increases, the maximum rate is reached at the optimum temperature.
	- As temperature increases beyond the optimal level, the rate of reaction decreases due to the active site changing shape, denaturing the enzyme
2. pH
	- The optimum pH is the pH the enzyme reaction rate is highest
	- If it's too acidic, H<sup>+</sup> ions break ionic/hydrogen bonds and denature enzymes
	- If it's too basic, OH<sup>-</sup> break ionic/hydrogen bonds and denature enzymes
3. Substrate concentration
	- Lower concentration: the reaction rate increase linearly due to many free enzyme active sites available
	- Increase in concentration: it reaches the **saturation point**—nearly all the enzymes are occupied. This means that the **enzyme concentration becomes the limiting factor** for more reactions. Thus, more enzymes are needed.
4. Enzyme concentration
	- Increases in concentration: more active sites for reaction 
	- As concentration increases, reaction rate does not increase anymore as every substrate is already being worked on by an enzyme. In this case, the **substrate concentration becomes the limiting factor** for more reactions. Thus, more substrates are needed.

# Inhibition & Cofactors 
## Inhibition
> [!question] What are inhibitors?
> These are molecules that bind to enzyme to reduce their activity.

### Type based on interaction
1. Reversible: inhibitors form weak bonds with the enzyme which can be detached to allow the enzyme to work again
2. Irreversible: inhibitors form strong bonds which cannot be detached 

### Type based on where they bind 
1. Competitive
	- Similar shape to active site
	- Binds directly to the active site where the substrate normally is 
	- Can be overcome by increasing substrate concentration
	- ![[competitive-inhibitor-enzyme-substrate-diagram.png]]
2. Non-competitive
	- Binds to an allosteric site, which causes the active site to change shape 
	- Substrate cannot bind due to incompatible shape 
	- Cannot be overcome by increasing substrate concentration
